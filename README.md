# Thaekoryzhal
The blind astronomer keeps a winter folded inside a copper eye.

A Window Facing Inward
The observatory stands in a valley where the stars are visible at noon. Its windows face inward. From outside, their glass shows an empty room. From inside, each window reveals a different valley, and in every valley a different observatory stands beneath the same pale sky.
The astronomer has been blind for as long as anyone remembers. He measures the heavens by placing his hands on the walls. At certain hours the stones become warm in the shape of constellations. He traces these shapes with a copper stylus and records them on thin sheets of black wax.
His records contain no dates. Each sheet is marked instead with a description of snow: snow on a closed gate, snow beneath a sleeping animal, snow seen through the eyes of somebody who has never known winter.
One sheet is marked with the words snow before the first cold. Nobody has found it among the others, although its place on the shelf is never empty.
The Eye in the Drawer
The astronomer's desk contains a drawer that opens only while a cloud covers the sun. Inside rests a copper eye, larger than a human eye and hollow at its center. Its pupil is a small square of folded white cloth.
He unfolds the cloth once a year. As he works, frost spreads across the desk. Within the cloth are mountains too small to name, valleys narrow as creases, and a frozen river that winds around the impressions of his fingers.
The river contains a boat. The boat contains a woman wearing a red coat. She has not moved since the astronomer first opened the drawer. Nevertheless, her coat grows longer each year, trailing behind the boat beneath the ice.
He never unfolds the cloth completely. There is always one crease left untouched, and the frost is always thickest around it.
A Visitor Who Remembered the River
A man arrived with a jar of warm snow. He had carried it across the valley without seeing the observatory until his shadow touched its door. The astronomer welcomed him by a name the man had forgotten in childhood.
They placed the jar beside the copper eye. The snow rose slowly inside the glass. When it reached the lid, the man's shadow climbed the wall and pressed itself against an inward window.
Through that window appeared the river. Its ice was clear, and the woman in the boat had turned her face toward the observatory. The man recognized her hands. He could remember those hands tying a ribbon around his wrist, but could remember neither the ribbon's color nor the reason it had been tied.
The astronomer asked him to open the jar. He refused. Behind the glass, the snow continued rising through a space that had no room left in it.
The Shape Between Constellations
There is a shape in the astronomer's records that appears only between other shapes. It resembles a wing when the wax is held horizontally, a mountain when held upright, and an open mouth when examined beneath a flame.
The astronomer calls it the Sleeper's Elbow. He says it belongs to a creature lying across the valleys visible through the windows. Its scales are the observatories. Its breath is the interval between their stars.
When the creature moves, one window becomes dark. In the valley beyond that window, the sky is said to remain unchanged, but every warm object remembers being cold. Bread develops a crust of frost. A handprint stays visible on a cup long after the cup is emptied.
The copper eye grows heavier during these movements. The astronomer balances it against a single black feather, adding nothing to either side. The balance remains level, though the desk beneath it bends.
Writing Beneath the Frost
The visitor stayed for seven nights. On the seventh, he saw words forming beneath the frost on the drawer:
Thae — kory — zhal — umrien.
He traced them with one finger. In the frozen river, the woman's boat moved a short distance upstream. The ribbon he could not remember appeared around his wrist, pale blue and warm as breath.
He finally opened the jar. The snow rose out of it in a narrow column and entered the folded cloth. For a moment the observatory was filled with the sound of boots crossing a wooden bridge. Then the cloth closed around the copper eye without being touched.
The ribbon loosened. The visitor's shadow returned from the window and lay beside his feet. It was now the shadow of an older man.
The Unfinished Measurement
Before leaving, the visitor asked what the astronomer had been measuring all these years. The astronomer placed the black feather in his palm. It was lighter than expected. Along its edge ran a line of tiny windows, each showing an empty boat beneath the ice.
The visitor looked toward the desk. The copper eye was gone. In its place lay a small red coat, folded with care, and beneath the coat a sheet of wax marked snow before the first cold.
The astronomer returned to the wall and rested both hands against the stone. Outside, the midday stars faded one by one. Inside, a single window began to show a valley in spring.
The last crease remains folded.
The last constellation remains unmeasured.
And the winter in the drawer has begun to dream of a river that can move.
